# Output for 03-tenant-caused

## Elements

1. **Existence of the warranty of habitability in the tenancy.** RPL § 235-b(1) implies the warranty in every residential lease, written or oral. [repo + secondary]
2. **A condition that breaches the warranty** — i.e., the premises are not fit for human habitation, not fit for the uses reasonably intended, or occupants are subjected to conditions dangerous, hazardous or detrimental to life, health or safety. RPL § 235-b(1); Park West, 47 N.Y.2d 316 (test: whether, in the eyes of a reasonable person, defects deprive the tenant of essential functions a residence is expected to provide; insect/rodent infestation, insufficient heat and plumbing, dangerous wiring, inadequate sanitation are examples). [opinion]
3. **The condition was not caused by the tenant or persons under the tenant's direction or control.** RPL § 235-b(1) statutory exception (as quoted in Park West, 47 N.Y.2d at 325 n.1); HMC § 27-2006 (tenant duties do not relieve owner obligations, but tenant-caused conditions are a defense). [opinion/repo]
4. **The condition is not de minimis or merely aesthetic.** Park West (a code violation may be "de minimis or ha[ve] no impact upon habitability"); Solow v. Wellner, 86 N.Y.2d 582 (objective minimum standard tied to essential residential functions). [opinion]
5. **Notice to the landlord and failure to repair within a reasonable time** — part of what mattered in Haskins (100 W 174 LLC v Haskins, 2014 NY Slip Op 51673(U)) and required for repair-and-deduct under Jangla Realty Co. v Gravagna, 112 Misc. 2d 642 (Civ. Ct. N.Y. Co. 1981). Note: Park West "does not discuss tenant notice as an element." [repo/opinion]
6. **For the remedy of abatement:** proof of the difference between fair market value as warranted (measured by rent reserved) and value during the breach, weighing severity, duration, and the landlord's remedial efforts. Park West; Solow. [opinion]
7. **For repair-and-deduct (if pursued):** (a) emergency seriously affecting habitability, (b) landlord refused or failed to repair after reasonable notice and opportunity, (c) condition cannot reasonably await code enforcement, (d) reasonable cost. Jangla. [opinion, persuasive only]

## How the law applies

- **Element 1 (warranty exists):** The narrative describes a residential tenancy with rent of $1,600. Nothing suggests a commercial lease (contrast Eastside Exhibition Corp. v. 210 East 86th St. Corp., 18 N.Y.3d 617 (2012), which is a commercial actual-partial-eviction case and does not address § 235-b). This element is likely satisfied, though the lease type is not stated.
- **Element 2 (breach condition):** The leaking toilet, soft floor, and smell could implicate "plumbing" and "inadequate sanitation" — categories Park West lists as habitability-affecting. A soft floor and persistent leak may also be "dangerous, hazardous or detrimental to life, health or safety" under § 235-b(1). **Hurts:** the hole in the wall was caused by the boyfriend's punch, and the cracked toilet tank by his kick — those specific conditions fall within the tenant-caused exception (Element 3). The tenant must separate the *leak/soft floor/smell* (arguably landlord's responsibility once it persists) from the *hole and cracked tank* (tenant-caused).
- **Element 3 (not tenant-caused):** This is the tenant's biggest problem. The tenant admits the boyfriend punched the wall and kicked/cracked the toilet tank. Under RPL § 235-b(1)'s exception and HMC § 27-2006, a condition "caused by the misconduct of the tenant or lessee or persons under his direction or control" is not a breach. The boyfriend's conduct is likely attributable to the tenant as a person under her direction or control. **Helps:** the tenant says she told the landlord about the leak *after it started* and he hasn't sent anyone — but if the leak originated from the tenant-caused cracked tank, the landlord may argue the entire condition is tenant-caused. The soft floor and smell may be *consequences* of the unrepaired leak; whether those consequences are attributable to the tenant's failure to mitigate or to the landlord's failure to repair is a factual question the narrative does not resolve.
- **Element 4 (not de minimis):** A two-week leak causing a soft floor and smell is plausibly more than de minimis, but the narrative gives no square footage, no indication the apartment is unusable, and no health impact. Solow requires an objective, essential-function standard. **Hurts:** two weeks is a short duration compared to Haskins (over a year) and Park West (17 days but severe, city-declared health emergency).
- **Element 5 (notice and failure to repair):** The tenant says she told the landlord about the leak after it started and he hasn't sent anyone. That is notice plus inaction — helpful under Haskins and Jangla. **Hurts:** the narrative does not say *how* she told him (oral? text? dated?), when exactly, or whether the landlord had a reasonable opportunity. Jangla found 18 hours sufficient for an exigent lock; two weeks of inaction after notice is likely ample opportunity, but the notice date is unstated.
- **Element 6 (abatement amount):** Rent is $1,600, which allows conversion of a percentage to dollars. But the narrative lacks duration precision (leak "for two weeks"), severity detail, and any evidence of the landlord's remedial efforts beyond "hasn't sent anyone."
- **Element 7 (repair-and-deduct):** The tenant has not said she made or paid for any repair, so this remedy is not yet in play. Jangla requires an emergency, notice, opportunity, and reasonable cost.

## Likely remedy

The most likely available remedy is a **rent abatement as a defense or setoff in a nonpayment proceeding**, not a full rent stoppage. Park West authorizes a percentage reduction of rent as a setoff where the tenant counterclaims or pleads breach as a defense; the fact-finder weighs severity, duration, and the landlord's remedial efforts. Solow confirms the standard is objective and tied to essential functions.

**However, the tenant-causation exception (RPL § 235-b(1); HMC § 27-2006) substantially undercuts the claim.** Because the tenant admits the boyfriend caused the hole and cracked the tank, the landlord will argue the entire condition is excluded. The tenant's best path is to frame the *leak's consequences* (soft floor, smell) as conditions the landlord failed to abate after notice — but the narrative does not establish that those consequences are independent of the tenant-caused damage.

**Abatement range:** The materials support percentages from roughly 1% to 40% (Park West 10%; Solow 10% plus 1–11%; Haskins 40% for 14 months). Given (a) the tenant-causation problem, (b) the short two-week duration, (c) the absence of documented notice, HPD violations, or health impact, and (d) the bathroom-only scope, a **low-end range of approximately 5%–15%** is the most defensible if breach is established at all — and **0% is a realistic outcome** if the court finds the condition entirely tenant-caused or de minimis. A 40% Haskins-type award is not supportable on these facts (no year-long chronic infestation, no documented violations, no established notice-and-failure timeline). The range is reasoning from the case outcomes in the materials, not a prediction.

**On stopping rent:** The tenant cannot simply stop paying. Withholding rent without documentation does not prevent a nonpayment suit; the tenant must prove breach and abatement, and failing risks judgment for full rent and eviction exposure. Full payment of arrears at any point up to eviction cures a nonpayment case (RPAPL § 731/§ 749(3)), and a defective rent demand can defeat it (RPAPL § 711(2); written 14-day demand requirement). [secondary]

**Repair-and-deduct** under Jangla is theoretically available if the tenant pays for a repair and the conditions are met, but she has not stated she did so, and the tenant-causation issue may defeat the "landlord refused to repair" element.
